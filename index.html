Read this once

Why you forget, and how this page fixes it

Forgetting after reading is normal; it isn't a bad memory. Reading feels like learning, but the brain keeps what it has to pull back out, and it keeps it longer each time you pull it out just before you would have forgotten. So this page works in three steps: understand it in plain words, attach a short hook, then let the flashcards ask you on day 1, 2, 4, 7, 15 and 30.

1 · Picture firstEvery topic starts with an everyday picture: Kafka is a newspaper archive, a circuit breaker is the fuse box at home. Learn the picture first; the technical words hang on it.
2 · Say it out loudBefore you flip a flashcard, answer out loud as if to an interviewer. If you can't say it, you don't know it yet. That's fine; press "Forgot".
3 · Trust the scheduleDon't re-read everything. Do the cards the page gives you each day. Cards you know well come back rarely; weak ones come back often.
4 · Teach a juniorAfter a topic, explain it in 60 seconds to an imaginary junior, or record a voice note on your phone and listen on the bus.
5 · Find it in your codeOpen your project and find a real @Transactional, a Hikari setting, a Redis key. Memory sticks to things you've touched.
6 · Short and dailyTwo 25-minute blocks beat one 3-hour block. Sleep is when memory is saved, so don't skip it before an interview.

First 2 minutes of every interview

Your self-introduction

Fill the yellow blanks once, then say it out loud every day until it sounds natural. Keep it under 2 minutes. End on what you want next, so the interviewer's first question comes from your strongest area.

Stories you still need to prepare

Every story above is an incident or a fix. Interviewers also ask these, so write one real example for each (3–4 lines, STAR):

A mistake with a real cost

What broke because of you, how you fixed it, what you changed so it can't recur.

Conflict with a business person

Pressure to cut a corner; how you explained the risk and what you agreed.

Unclear requirement

The PRD validation that found false premises is a strong one.

Ownership beyond your role

The environment cleanup, or the review/test automation you built.

A trade-off you'd reverse

What you chose then, what you learned, what you'd do now.

Mentoring with an outcome

Who, what you did, how they grew. Names not needed.

Probe questions to rehearse for every story

Round 2 is mostly follow-ups. Answer each of these for each story, out loud:

  • Why did you choose that approach, and what else did you consider?
  • What numbers show the impact (users affected, money, latency, time saved)?
  • How did you verify the fix worked?
  • What would you do differently now?
  • What did you add so it can't happen again (test, alert, process)?
  • What was your part, and what did others do?

Kafka and AWS show up in many question lists. If you haven't run them in production, say so plainly, then explain the concepts well. "We used Redis and DB-backed queues; with Kafka I'd do it like this…" lands better than bluffing.

Getting the offer

Job-hunt checklist

Resume

  • One or two pages. Every bullet: action + technology + result, e.g. "Built an outbox-based transfer orchestrator in Spring Boot, removing double-posting on timeouts".
  • Put the keywords recruiters search for: Java 17/21, Spring Boot, microservices, PostgreSQL, Redis, Keycloak/OAuth2/OIDC, Docker, payments.
  • The top third of page one gets read; put fintech and payments there.

Applying

  • Target payments, fintech, banks and banking-software vendors. Your domain experience is the strongest card you have.
  • Referrals beat cold applications. Message two or three engineers per company you like, short and specific.
  • LinkedIn: headline "Java Backend Engineer · Spring Boot · Payments/Fintech", "Open to work" visible to recruiters only. Your own posts already help.
  • Keep a simple tracker: company, date, stage, next step.

In the room

  • "Let me think for a moment" is fine. Silence for 10 seconds is better than a wrong rush.
  • Don't know? Say how you'd find out: "I haven't used that, but I'd check X, then Y."
  • Scenario questions: walk the 6 steps (Scope, Measure, Locate, Prove, Mitigate, Prevent) before naming a fix.
  • System design: ask about volume and consistency before drawing.

Questions to ask them

  • "What does a normal week look like for this team?"
  • "How do you deploy, and how often do things break in production?"
  • "What would make someone successful here in the first 90 days?"
  • Salary: research the band first. If asked, give a range whose bottom you'd be happy with.

Practice with a human voice too. In Claude Code, type something like "mock interview me on Kafka" or "mock interview: @Transactional, 10 questions, be strict". I'll ask one question at a time, wait for your answer, and tell you what an interviewer would mark down.
